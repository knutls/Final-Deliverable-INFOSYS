\subsection{Case 1}
This section compares the AS-IS model for recording and reviewing lectures (Figure~\ref{fig:case1}) with the TO-BE model (Figure~\ref{fig:case1-tobe}). The TO-BE process itself is described in Section~\ref{subsec: tobeCase1}. Here, the focus is on what the change means for the actors involved, how NTNU can carry it out, and what new challenges it introduces.

\textbf{Overview of changes:}
Table~\ref{tab:case1-changes} summarizes the main differences between the two models.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{|p{0.22\textwidth}|p{0.34\textwidth}|p{0.34\textwidth}|}
        \hline
        \textbf{Aspect} & \textbf{AS-IS} & \textbf{TO-BE} \\
        \hline
        Processing of the recording & None. Panopto only stores the raw video. & A GenAI service generates a transcript, a topic index and study material from each recording. \\
        \hline
        Navigation & Table of contents based on slide titles. The student scrubs through the video. & Searchable transcript and topic index with timestamps. The student jumps directly to the relevant segment. \\
        \hline
        Study material & Written from scratch by each student, for every lecture. & Generated once per lecture. The student reads it, tests themselves with quiz questions and adds own comments. \\
        \hline
        Teaching staff & Order the recording. & Order the recording, and review and approve the generated material before it is published. \\
        \hline
        Key student activity & Scrub through the full recording to find a concept. & Search for the concept and jump to the right timestamp. \\
        \hline
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Main changes from the AS-IS to the TO-BE model for Case 1}
    \label{tab:case1-changes}
\end{table}

\textbf{What the change means:}
Overall, the change moves the work of making lecture content usable from each individual student to an automated service. In the AS-IS model, every student had to find concepts by scrubbing through the video and write their own notes, and the informal walkthrough showed that a 90-minute lecture took about three hours to review. In the TO-BE model, this work is done once per lecture, and the student can spend the time on understanding the content rather than on finding it. The recording part of the process, where Panopto records and stores the lecture, is kept as it is, and the GenAI service is added after the recording has been stored. This limits the scope of the change, since a system that already works is not replaced. It also makes the transition less risky, as the raw video remains available, and students can fall back on the AS-IS way of working if the generated material is missing or of poor quality. The teaching staff get a new task, since they must approve the material before it is published.

\textbf{Carrying out the change:}
The transition can be structured using Lewin's change model of unfreezing, moving and refreezing \parencite{stair}. Before development starts, NTNU must ensure that the recordings are only processed by an AI service covered by a data processing agreement, and that they are not used to train the model. Lecturers must also consent to their recordings being transcribed and summarized, since the lecture content is their work. The pain points from the AS-IS model, and the time spent by students on reviewing a single lecture, can be used to show lecturers and NTNU adminiatration why the change is needed. The solution should then be piloted in a few large courses that are already recorded in Panopto, before it is rolled out to more courses. The launch should take place at the start of a semester, so that students and teaching staff can get used to the new material before the exam period.

\textbf{Changed roles:}
The teaching staff get the most new work, as they must review and approve the generated material for every lecture. This may be met with resistance, either because it is seen as an extra burden or from a lack of trust in the generated material. Following TAM, the review step will only be done properly if it is perceived as useful and easy to do \parencite{stair,toussaint2025}. It should therefore be integrated in Panopto or Canvas, where the lecturer can approve or edit the material directly, rather than offered as a separate tool. Lecturers should take part in the pilot and be told clearly that they remain responsible for the material published in their course. For the students, the role changes from producing study material from the lectures to using and completing it. Students should be informed that the material is AI-generated and approved by the lecturer. Students in the pilot courses and student representatives can act as early adopters, in line with s' diffusion of innovation.

\textbf{New challenges:}
The changes also introduce new challenges that NTNU must handle. Recordings contain the voice and image of the lecturer, and sometimes of students asking questions, which is personal data under GDPR and requires a legal basis and clear information. Speech-to-text can misinterpret technical terms or lectures that mix Norwegian and English, and an LLM can produce summaries and quiz questions that sound convincing but are wrong \parencite{qian2025}. The review step is meant to catch these errors, but under time pressure lecturers may approve the material without reading it properly, which would move the problem rather than solve it. There is also a risk that students rely too much on the generated summaries and read them passively, instead of engaging with the lecture, or that fewer students attend the lectures. The TO-BE model counters this by letting the students test themselves with quiz questions and add their own comments, but AI literacy is still a prerequisite. Students need to understand that the generated material can be wrong and should be checked against the recording. Finally, the GenAI service creates a new dependency, which requires someone to own the system and handle errors in the generated material over time.

\textbf{Evaluating the change:}
To know whether the change has succeeded, NTNU should measure the effects against the AS-IS situation. Relevant indicators are the time students spend on reviewing a lecture compared with the three hours in the AS-IS walkthrough, the amount of students using the transcript, topic index and quiz questions, the share of generated material that the lecturers need to adjust, and student satisfaction with the lecture material, which relates to the net benefits in the DeLone and McLean model \parencite{stair}. A very low adjustment rate combined with reports of errors in the material would indicate that it is being approved without review. When the results are satisfactory, the new process should be made the standard for all recorded lectures, with a clear system owner and routines for reporting and correcting errors.

\textbf{What the change means:} In the AS-IS model, whether a student received written feedback depended on TA workload rather than on what the student needed. In the TO-BE model, every student receives written feedback, and since all drafts are based on the same criteria, the feedback should be more consistent across TAs. The TA's responsibility for the assessment is kept, but the work moves from producing feedback to quality-assuring it. Canvas still collects the submissions and publishes the results, which limits the scope of the change and lets TAs fall back on the AS-IS way of working if the assistant fails.

Before development starts, NTNU must ensure that submissions are only processed by an AI service covered by a data processing agreement, and that they are not used to train the model. Course coordinators must write explicit assessment criteria for each exercise, since the drafts are never better than the basis they are generated from. The solution should then be piloted in a few large courses with frequent exercises before wider rollout.

\textbf{Changed roles:} The TAs are the actors whose daily work changes the most, as they go from assessing and writing to reviewing and approving. This may be met with resistance, either from a lack of trust in the drafts or from a feeling that the work becomes less meaningful. Following TAM, the TAs will only use the assistant if it is perceived as useful and easy to use (Stair and Reynolds 2018; Toussaint and Krogstie 2025). It should therefore be integrated in the Canvas grading view rather than offered as a separate tool. TAs should take part in the pilot and be told clearly that they remain responsible for every score and comment they approve. Students should be informed that the score and feedback are AI-assisted and approved by a TA.

\textbf{New challenges:} Student submissions are personal data, and processing them in an AI service requires a legal basis under GDPR and clear information to the students. An LLM can produce feedback that sounds convincing but is wrong, for example by marking a correct but unconventional solution as an error. Because the assistant also suggests a score, there is a risk that the TA anchors on it instead of making an independent assessment. Under time pressure, TAs may also approve drafts without reading them properly, which would move the quality problem rather than solve it. This makes AI literacy a prerequisite. TAs need enough understanding of how LLMs fail to recognize plausible but incorrect feedback, and students need to understand that AI-assisted feedback can be wrong and may be questioned. Finally, if many drafts need changes, the expected time saving will not be realized.

\textbf{Evaluating the change:} Success can be measured by the share of submissions receiving written feedback, TA time per submission, and student satisfaction, which relates to net benefits in the DeLone and McLean model (Stair and Reynolds 2018). A very low adjustment rate combined with complaints about feedback quality would indicate that drafts are being approved without review.

\subsection{Case 1: Lecture recordings}

\textbf{Scope of the change:}
In the AS-IS model (Figure~\ref{fig:case1}), the recording process is fully automated and ends when Panopto has stored the video. The TO-BE model (Figure~\ref{fig:case1-tobe}) keeps this part unchanged and adds the GenAI service after the event \textit{Recording stored}. This makes Case 1 the least disruptive of the three changes. Nothing that works today is replaced, and the raw video is still published, so students can fall back on the AS-IS way of working if the generated material is missing or wrong. %It is a typical sustaining innovation, where an existing service is improved rather than replaced \parencite{toussaint2025}.

\textbf{The lecturer's perspective:}
The lecturer gets the largest new responsibility. In the AS-IS model, the lecturer only ordered the recording. In the TO-BE model, the lecturer must also approve a transcript, summary and quiz questions that are published in their course. Lecturers already worry that recordings reduce attendance and engagement \parencite{ocallaghan2017}, and ready-made summaries may make this worry stronger. To answer this concern, the material should be presented to lecturers as support for repetition after the lecture, not as a replacement for it, and attendance should be followed in the pilot courses so that the concern is checked rather than assumed. The lecture and slides are also the lecturer's own work, so NTNU needs their consent before the service processes them. Since the recordings also contain the voices of students who ask questions, the service must be covered by a data processing agreement that prevents the recordings from being used to train the model. Consent and the agreement are the prerequisites for Case 1, and must be in place before the pilot starts. For lecturers to accept the extra task, approval has to be quick, for example a single view in Panopto or Canvas where the material can be read, corrected and approved.

\textbf{Rollout:}
Lecturers will not adopt the solution at the same pace. Rogers' diffusion of innovation describes how innovators and early adopters take up a new solution first, followed by the majority and finally the laggards \parencite{stair}. Instead of enabling the service for all recorded lectures at once, NTNU should let lecturers opt in, starting with a few large recorded courses where the lecturers are positive. Results from these courses, such as how much time students save, can then be used to convince the majority. The service should only be offered to all recorded courses when the time spent on reviewing one lecture is clearly lower than the three hours measured in the AS-IS walkthrough, and learning outcomes in the pilot courses are not worse. The material should be available from the first lecture of the semester, so that students learn to use it well before the exam period.

\textbf{Challenges:}
For the students, the risk is that the material replaces the lecture instead of supporting it. GenAI summaries can contain errors that sound convincing, and overreliance can weaken students' own critical thinking \parencite{qian2025}. The TO-BE model limits this in two ways. The transcript and topic index are linked to timestamps, so the student can check a summary against what the lecturer actually said, and the quiz questions and own comments keep the student working with the content. This still requires some AI literacy, as students must know that the material is generated and can be wrong. The material should therefore be clearly marked as AI-generated, and the lecturer can show how to use it in the first lecture. Errors can also come from the transcription itself, since speech-to-text can misinterpret technical terms and lectures that mix Norwegian and English. The lecturer's review is meant to catch these errors, but under time pressure the material may be approved without being read, which moves the problem rather than solving it. During the pilot, NTNU should therefore follow how often lecturers correct the material and how many errors students report. A very low correction rate combined with many reported errors would indicate that the review has become a formality. 


\subsection{Case 2: Feedback on exercises}

\textbf{The task:}
The structure of the TA's work is kept. Both the AS-IS model (Figure~\ref{fig:case2}) and the TO-BE model (Figure~\ref{fig:case2tobe}) loop through the submissions one at a time, and the TA still decides the score. What changes is the task inside the loop. The TA starts from an AI draft instead of an empty comment field, and the gateway \textit{Sufficient time to make a comment?} is replaced by \textit{Changes needed?}. Written feedback is therefore no longer what gets cut when time runs out. The change is also limited to exercises in Canvas, which in Norwegian higher education is used for ungraded assessment, while graded exams run in dedicated systems such as Inspera \parencite{chirumamilla2021}. The final grade, which is set by the external sensor, is not affected, and this makes exercises a suitable place to introduce AI-assisted assessment.

\textbf{Prerequisites:}
In the AS-IS model, the TA checked each answer against the solution proposal. The GenAI service also needs explicit assessment criteria, since a draft is never better than the basis it is generated from. Writing these criteria is a new task for the course coordinator, and it must be done when the exercise is published, before any submission arrives. Course coordinators may see this as extra work, but criteria can be reused when the same exercise is given the next year, so most of the effort comes in the first semester. An exercise without clear criteria should keep the AS-IS process until such criteria are written. Submissions are also personal data, and they can only be sent to the GenAI service under the data processing agreement established in Case 1. Students must be told before the course starts that their submissions are processed by AI, and that a TA approves every score and comment.

\textbf{Changing the routine:}
TAs assess exercises the same way every week, so the change means breaking an established routine. Some TAs may resist it because they distrust the drafts, or because checking AI output feels less meaningful than assessing on their own. Lewin's change model fits this well \parencite{stair}. In the unfreezing phase, the AS-IS pain point that many students only receive a score is used to show TAs and course coordinators why the routine should change. In the moving phase, the assistant is piloted in a few large courses with frequent exercises. Following TAM, TAs will only use it if it is perceived as useful and easy to use, so it should be built into the Canvas grading view they already work in. Refreezing is harder than usual, because most TAs are hired for one semester at a time. Guidelines for reviewing AI drafts, including how LLMs typically fail, must therefore be part of the TA training every semester, not something that is introduced once. This is the main measure against a lack of AI literacy in Case 2, since TAs must be able to recognize feedback that sounds plausible but is wrong.

\textbf{Challenges:}
The main risk is that the review step becomes a formality. GenAI can produce feedback that sounds right but is wrong or biased \parencite{qian2025}, for example by marking a correct but unconventional solution as an error. Because the draft also suggests a score, the TA may anchor on it rather than assess the submission independently, especially with many submissions left. To detect this, the course coordinator should check a sample of approved submissions after each round and follow how often TAs change the drafts. If almost no drafts are changed while students complain about the feedback, drafts are likely being approved unread. Students should be able to see that the feedback is AI-assisted, and ask the TA about it if it seems wrong. The change has succeeded if every student receives written feedback without the TA spending more time per submission than today.


\subsection{Case 3: Course selection}

\textbf{Complexity:}
In Cases 1 and 2, the GenAI service works on content that already passes through the process, a recording or a submission. The course recommender (Figure~\ref{fig:case3-tobe-b}) instead needs data that is not connected to course selection today, like grades and completed courses from FS, and course descriptions from the course pages. In the AS-IS model (Figure~\ref{fig:case3}), these sources were only brought together by the student's own manual research. Since an information system consists of data, people and procedures as well as software \parencite{toussaint2025}, the recommender requires new data flows and new routines for keeping the data correct. Case 3 is therefore the most demanding transition of the three, even though only two tasks change in the student's own process (Figure~\ref{fig:case3-tobe-a}).

\textbf{Prerequisites:}
Three things must be in place before development starts. First, NTNU needs a legal basis under GDPR for using grades to recommend courses, and an agreement with Sikt, which runs FS, about access to the data. Grades are the most sensitive data in the solution, so the recommender should only retrieve what it needs, such as completed courses and relevant grades, and students must be informed about how their data is used. Second, the eligibility pre-check should use the same prerequisite and admission rules that Studentweb already uses to validate registrations. If the rules differ, the recommender may suggest a course that Studentweb then rejects, which would undo one of the main benefits. Third, the course descriptions must be complete and up to date. In the DeLone and McLean model, information quality affects both use and net benefits \parencite{stair}, and recommendations need accurate course descriptions to work. Updating the descriptions must therefore become a fixed routine for the faculties each semester.

\textbf{Role changes and uptake:}
In the AS-IS model, the study advisor was the only source of personal guidance. In the TO-BE model, the recommender handles the first round, and the advisor uses the same service to prepare answers (Figure~\ref{fig:case3-tobe-c}). Advisors may see this as a threat to their role or distrust the suggestions, so they should take part in the pilot and learn where the service fails. Their role also shifts from answering every question to handling the cases where the AI is not enough, which can leave more time for students with complex study plans.

For the students, the recommender only creates value if they use it. UTAUT adds social influence and facilitating conditions to the factors in TAM \parencite{stair}. Students are more likely to try the recommender if advisors and fellow students point them to it, and if it is available where they already are. Since the subprocess starts with the student logging in to Studentweb with Feide, the recommender should be placed there and not in a separate tool. 

\textbf{Rollout and challenges:}
Course selection is concentrated in short periods with fixed deadlines, so a failure during registration hits many students at once. The pilot should run in a few study programmes with many electives and be launched well before a registration period, with the course pages and advisors still available as a fallback. A risk specific to this case is that the recommender steers students. Recommendations based on grades may push some groups away from demanding courses, and courses that are often recommended may become crowded. The explanations attached to each recommendation make it possible to see why a course was suggested, and the student still makes the final choice. The opposite risk is that students follow the recommendations without reflecting on them. Students should therefore be told that the recommendations are suggestions based on their data and can be wrong, both in Studentweb and by the advisors before the registration period. During the pilot, NTNU should check whether different groups of students receive systematically different recommendations. Success can be measured by fewer rejected registrations, fewer and faster inquiries to the study advisors, and whether students are satisfied with the courses they chose.
